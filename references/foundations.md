# Foundations: choose a lens for the problem

These are compact reference cards. Source descriptions are bounded to the material inspected. The working questions and applications below are original Miles prompts, not quotations from the books or numbered lens cards. Full source details and access limits are in [sources.md](sources.md).

## Experience intent — MDA

**Reference:** Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, Robert Zubek, *MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research* (2004). [REF-01](sources.md#ref-01)

The paper relates mechanics, runtime dynamics, and the desired experience it calls aesthetics. Here “aesthetics” reaches beyond visual style.

**Use when:** a proposed mechanic has an unclear relationship to the desired experience.

**Miles asks:** What does the player do? What behavior might these rules create over time? What experience is that behavior intended to support? What unintended pattern could undermine it?

**Boundary:** A conceptual relationship supplies a hypothesis, not proof that a player will feel a particular emotion.

## Discoverability and consequence — Don Norman

**Reference:** *The Design of Everyday Things*, revised and expanded edition. The author's overview and contents describe signifiers, mapping, feedback, conceptual models, and the gulfs between intention, action, and evaluation. [REF-02](sources.md#ref-02)

**Use when:** players struggle to find an action, operate a control, or understand an outcome.

**Miles asks:** What communicates the available action? What outcome would the learned mapping suggest? What evidence lets the player assess the result?

**Boundary:** Interface clarity should preserve the discovery or challenge the game deliberately creates. The linked material is an author overview, contents, and preface, not the full book.

## Multiple perspectives — Jesse Schell

**Reference:** *The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses*. The official studio page introduces its collection of design perspectives. [REF-03](sources.md#ref-03)

**Use when:** a decision is being evaluated from only one angle.

**Miles asks:** How does the same choice affect the player, rules, fiction, interface, and production? Which perspective reveals a consequential cost?

**Boundary:** Select a few relevant perspectives. This project neither reproduces the lens deck nor attributes these original questions to numbered lenses.

## Learning and mastery — Raph Koster

**Reference:** *A Theory of Fun for Game Design*. The author's published excerpt discusses changing interest as familiar play becomes predictable. [REF-04](sources.md#ref-04)

**Use when:** repeated play seems to offer little new understanding or mastery.

**Miles asks:** What can the player learn or express on the next pass? Does variation create a new decision? What value remains after the operation becomes familiar?

**Boundary:** Learning is one lens. Social connection, expression, atmosphere, relaxation, and other experiences can matter independently. The inspected excerpt supports a narrow reading, not a complete summary of the book.

## Player-centered iteration — Tracy Fullerton

**Reference:** *Game Design Workshop*. Its official site describes playcentric design, experience goals, prototyping, playtesting, and revision. [REF-05](sources.md#ref-05)

**Use when:** a design debate needs a concrete way to learn.

**Miles asks:** Which uncertainty matters most? What prototype makes it observable? What result would change the design?

**Boundary:** The official overview establishes the approach; detailed book exercises are not reproduced here.

## Interface inspection — Jakob Nielsen

**Reference:** *10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design*. The author organization's guide addresses status visibility, consistency, recognition, and error recovery among its principles. [REF-06](sources.md#ref-06)

**Use when:** reviewing a HUD, menu, configuration flow, or transaction.

**Miles asks:** Can the player tell the current state, recognize valid choices, predict a familiar control, and recover from a mistake?

**Boundary:** Heuristic inspection generates issues to investigate. It establishes neither observed player behavior nor the quality of the game's intentional challenge.

## Empirical design — Valve

**Reference:** Mike Ambinder, *Valve's Approach to Playtesting: The Application of Empiricism* (GDC 2009). The talk presents design iteration as hypothesis, observation, and revision. [REF-07](sources.md#ref-07)

**Use when:** a strong design opinion is being treated as a demonstrated player outcome.

**Miles asks:** What would we need to observe? What could explain the same behavior differently? How will assistance or measurement change the session?

**Boundary:** Session observations, telemetry, and self-reports have different limitations. Keep each conclusion within its evidence.

## Accessible signals — Xbox Accessibility Guidelines

**Reference:** XAG 103 covers alternative channels for essential visual and audio information. [REF-08](sources.md#ref-08)

**Use when:** a design relies on a particular sensory channel or color cue.

**Miles asks:** What happens with audio muted, a cue's color unavailable, or haptics disabled? Which equivalent signal preserves the intended information?

**Boundary:** This card helps identify requirements to inspect. It supplies no compliance certification or substitute for testing with relevant players.

## Selecting a reference

Choose the uncertainty first, then a reference that helps investigate it. For a simple question, one lens may be sufficient. Combine a lens with a [classic case](../examples/README.md) only when the case shares a meaningful mechanism with the current problem.

When a detailed factual claim requires more than these summaries, inspect the linked primary material or the relevant game build. Report the exact access boundary. Keep inaccessible content explicitly unverified.
