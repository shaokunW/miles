# Onboarding and learning transfer

Use this guide when players encounter a game, system, control, or changed rule for the first time.

## Start with the experience promise

Define the first meaningful experience the project wants to deliver: solving a small problem, serving a customer, making a tactical choice, discovering a place, or sharing a moment. Connect instruction to that purpose.

Identify the knowledge required for the next useful action. Track it explicitly:

| Concept | Prerequisite | First use | Evidence of understanding | Help available later |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fill from the current project | Learned control or concept | A meaningful situation | An observable independent action | Contextual recovery route |

Keep this model separate from the save data that records tutorial completion. A completion flag alone says little about what the player understood.

## A candidate learning sequence

1. **Encounter a reason to act.** Make a relevant problem or opportunity perceivable.
2. **Attempt with suitable support.** Expose the required control and permit low-cost exploration.
3. **Read the consequence.** Make the action-result connection available in the game's language.
4. **Use the idea in a variation.** Change something that requires understanding beyond repeating a highlighted tap.
5. **Act with reduced assistance.** Observe transfer, while preserving a route to help.

Adapt or shorten the sequence to the player and mechanic. There is no mandatory number of repetitions, fixed duration, or universal rule that only one action can be visible. A deliberately restricted opening needs a scoped project decision.

## Choose assistance deliberately

Use existing controls and the relevant communication channels. An environmental cue can support discovery; a concise label can remove a costly ambiguity; a demonstration can show timing; optional explanation can serve unfamiliar players.

When adding escalation, specify its trigger and its reset conditions. Inactivity may indicate observation, distraction, an access need, or uncertainty. Combine dwell time with repeated ineffective attempts or an explicit help request where the design permits it. Tune timing through observation instead of declaring an arbitrary universal threshold.

Provide accessible equivalents for essential information. Keep assistance consistent with the game's settings and input device. A pointer animation that assumes touch needs a different treatment for a controller.

## Transfer and returning players

Test whether players can identify an equivalent opportunity without the original highlight. A novel layout or changed object can reveal memorized sequence-following.

Define skipped, replayed, interrupted, and partially completed onboarding. Save at stable transitions; avoid charging or granting twice after resuming. A returning player may need a short goal reminder rather than the entire original lesson.

The onboarding handoff should describe eligibility, player-visible states, input, transitions, success, failure, recovery, and the next independent action. When implementation matters, ask the engineering team to confirm save and transaction semantics.

## Measure the actual question

For learnability, observe independent completion, mistaken attempts, requests for help, and the player's account of what happened. A faster completion can coexist with weaker understanding. For enjoyment or retention, use evidence designed for those questions.
